Chien-Shiung Wu was born in Liuhe, near Shanghai. Her father had founded one of the first schools for girls in the region. She studied physics in Nanjing, sailed to the United States in 1936, and earned her doctorate at Berkeley in 1940.[^3]

During the war she joined the Manhattan Project at Columbia University. She became known as one of the most exacting experimenters in nuclear physics, particularly on beta decay.[^3]

## The question nobody had tested

In 1956, the theorists Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang asked whether nature might tell left from right in weak nuclear interactions. Physicists had assumed for decades that it could not. Lee and Yang pointed out that no one had actually checked.

Wu designed the experiment. She cooled radioactive cobalt-60 to near absolute zero at the National Bureau of Standards, aligned the nuclei in a magnetic field, and measured which way the electrons came out. They favoured one direction. Parity, a supposed law of physics, was broken.[^1]

## The prize

Within months of her result, the 1957 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Lee and Yang, for their theoretical work on parity.[^2] The experiment that proved them right was not part of the prize.

## Afterwards

Wu went on to confirm other predictions of the weak interaction and to study the molecular basis of sickle-cell disease. In 1975 she became the first woman president of the American Physical Society. In 1978 she received the first Wolf Prize in Physics.[^3]
