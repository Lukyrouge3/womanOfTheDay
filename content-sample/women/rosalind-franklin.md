Rosalind Franklin was born in London and studied chemistry at Cambridge. Her doctoral work on the structure of coal fed straight into Britain's wartime industry. In Paris she then became an expert in X-ray diffraction, the technique of reading a molecule's shape from the patterns it scatters.[^1]

## King's College

In 1951 she joined King's College London to study DNA. With her student Raymond Gosling, she produced the sharpest X-ray images of DNA yet made. One of them, Photo 51, showed the unmistakable cross of a helix.[^1] [^2]

Her working relationship with her colleague Maurice Wilkins was hostile from the start. In early 1953, without her knowledge, Wilkins showed Photo 51 to James Watson. Watson and Francis Crick also saw her unpublished measurements in a research council report.[^1] [^4]

## The structure

Watson and Crick published their double helix in *Nature* in April 1953. Franklin and Gosling's paper appeared in the same issue, presented as supporting evidence.[^2] Historians now argue that she was an equal contributor to the discovery, not a bystander whose photograph was used.[^4]

## The prize

Franklin died of ovarian cancer in 1958, at 37. In 1962 the Nobel Prize went to Crick, Watson and Wilkins.[^3] Nobel Prizes are not awarded after death. Six years later, Watson's bestselling memoir, *The Double Helix*, portrayed her as a difficult assistant he called "Rosy", a caricature that shaped her reputation for decades.[^1]

## Afterwards

At Birkbeck College she led pioneering work on the structure of viruses, published a steady stream of papers in her last five years, and trained the team that carried it on after her death.[^1]
