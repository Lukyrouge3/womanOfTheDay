Lise Meitner grew up in Vienna at a time when Austrian girls could not attend the schools that prepared boys for university. She studied privately, passed the entrance exam at 22, and in 1906 became one of the first women to earn a doctorate in physics at the University of Vienna.[^1]

## Berlin, through the back door

In 1907 she moved to Berlin and began a thirty-year partnership with the chemist Otto Hahn. Women were not allowed inside the chemistry institute where he worked, so Meitner was given a former carpentry workshop in the basement, with its own entrance. She was not paid for her first years there.[^1]

She rose anyway. She headed the physics department of the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Chemistry, co-discovered the element protactinium with Hahn, and in 1926 became the first woman in Germany to be appointed professor of physics.[^1]

## Exile, and the explanation

When Germany annexed Austria in 1938, Meitner, who was of Jewish descent, lost the protection of her Austrian passport. She escaped to Sweden in July with almost nothing.[^1]

That December, Hahn and Fritz Strassmann found barium in uranium bombarded with neutrons. They could not explain it, and Hahn wrote to Meitner. Walking in the snow with her nephew Otto Robert Frisch, she worked out what had happened: the uranium nucleus had split in two, releasing an enormous amount of energy. Their paper in *Nature* gave the process its name: fission.[^2]

## The prize

In November 1945, the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Hahn alone, for the discovery of the fission of heavy nuclei.[^3] Meitner had been nominated for Nobel Prizes many times. Historians who later studied the committee's files concluded that her exclusion came from disciplinary bias, the politics of the war years, and the committee's failure to understand her physics.[^4]

Hahn, for his part, came to describe the discovery as a chemist's alone.[^4]

## Afterwards

Meitner refused to work on the atomic bomb. She kept working in Sweden, then retired to Cambridge, where she died in 1968. In 1997, element 109 was named meitnerium in her honour, a recognition few people receive.[^1]
